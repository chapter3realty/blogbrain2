How a HECM for Purchase works

A HECM (Home Equity Conversion Mortgage) is the reverse mortgage insured by the Federal Housing Administration (FHA). Most people use one on a home they already own. A HECM for Purchase uses it to buy a new home instead.

The reverse mortgage pays part of the price at closing. You pay the rest from your own money. After closing, you own the home and make no monthly mortgage payment. The loan balance grows over time and is repaid when you sell, move out for good, or after the last borrower dies, usually from the sale of the home.

How much you bring to closing

You pay the difference between the price and the amount the reverse mortgage can lend, plus any loan costs you do not finance, minus your earnest money.

The amount the loan can lend depends on three things:

  • the age of the youngest borrower, or of an eligible spouse who is not on the loan; the older you are, the more the loan can lend;
  • current interest rates;
  • the home's price or appraised value, whichever is lower, up to $1,249,125 for loans started in 2026.

Because the share changes with age and rates, we give you the exact figure in writing for your age and price, not a rule of thumb.

An example, in words

A retired couple sells their home in Ohio and finds a house near Murrells Inlet. He is 74 and she is 71, so the loan is figured on her age, the younger of the two. They pay the rest from the sale of the Ohio house, close once, and move in the next week. They keep the rest of their savings, and they make no monthly mortgage payment. They still pay the property tax, the insurance and the HOA.

The rules you must meet

  • Age. The youngest borrower must be 62 or older at closing.
  • Counseling. Every borrower, and any non-borrowing spouse, meets with a HUD-approved counselor, who is independent of us and explains the loan, its costs and other choices. We need the counseling certificate to go ahead.
  • Your own money. Your share comes from cash you have or from selling your assets, such as your current home, or another source HUD approves. Money from the seller cannot be used for it.
  • Moving in. Every borrower must make the home their principal residence within 60 days of closing.
  • A financial check. The lender checks that you can keep up with taxes, insurance and upkeep, and may set some loan money aside for those bills.
  • An existing reverse mortgage. If you have one on your current home, it must be paid off before the new one closes.

HUD limits what the seller may pay: the fees a seller must pay by law, the fees sellers customarily pay locally, a home warranty, and anything else HUD allows by notice. A seller credit toward your share of the price is not allowed.

Which homes qualify

The home must be finished and ready to live in, with a certificate of occupancy for a new build. Serious safety or soundness problems must be repaired by the seller before closing.

A condo qualifies only if the building is acceptable to HUD under FHA's condo rules. Not every condo building is FHA-approved, so we check the building before you make an offer. Our guide to condo mortgage requirements explains what buildings must pass.

What you still pay after closing

No monthly mortgage payment is required. You still pay:

  • property taxes;
  • homeowners insurance, and flood insurance if the home is in a flood zone (see flood insurance and your mortgage);
  • HOA dues, if any;
  • repairs and upkeep.

If you fall behind on these, the loan can come due, and you could lose the home. The loan can also come due after more than 12 months in a row away from the home for illness. Interest and the FHA mortgage insurance premium are added to the loan balance each month, so the balance grows and the equity left for you or your heirs shrinks over time. You may make payments if you choose. To understand what happens later, read who is responsible for a reverse mortgage after death.

HECM for Purchase compared with paying cash

Pay cashHECM for Purchase
Cash needed at closingThe full pricePart of the price, plus unfinanced costs
Monthly mortgage paymentNoneNone required
Savings left after the moveLessMore
Loan costsNoneFHA insurance premium, interest and closing costs
Equity in the home laterAll of itLess, as the balance grows

A HECM for Purchase suits a buyer who wants to keep savings for health care, travel or family, and plans to stay in the home for years. It suits less well a buyer who may move again soon, because the loan costs come early.

The steps, from offer to move-in

  1. Estimate. We give you the amount you would bring at your age and price range.
  2. Counseling. You complete a session with a HUD-approved counselor.
  3. Offer. You make an offer, with the reverse mortgage named as your financing.
  4. Application and appraisal. We take your application, order the FHA appraisal and complete the financial check.
  5. Closing. You bring your share, sign once, and get the keys.
  6. Move in. Within 60 days.
