Yes, you can buy a house with a reverse mortgage. It's called a HECM for Purchase. If you are 62 or older, you pay part of the price yourself, the reverse mortgage pays the rest, and you don't have to make a monthly mortgage payment. You still pay property taxes, insurance and upkeep.

Most people use a reverse mortgage on a home they already own. HECM for Purchase is the version for buying your next one. HECM stands for Home Equity Conversion Mortgage, the reverse mortgage insured by the FHA. It suits people who are downsizing or retiring to the coast, and want to keep their savings in the bank.

How buying with a reverse mortgage works

  1. Bring your share. It usually comes from selling your current home, from savings, or both.
  2. Close once. The purchase and the reverse mortgage close together. There is no second loan to pay off later.
  3. Move in within 60 days. The home becomes your main home, with no monthly mortgage payment.

The loan is repaid when you sell the home, move out for good, or after the last borrower dies. It can be paid from the sale of the home.

An example. A retired couple sells their home in Ohio and buys a house near Murrells Inlet. He is 74 and she is 71, so the loan is based on her age, the younger of the two. The reverse mortgage pays part of the price, and they pay the rest from the sale of the Ohio house. They close once and move in the next week. Their other savings stay in the bank, and there is no mortgage bill each month. They still pay the property tax, insurance, HOA dues and upkeep.

How much do you need for a HECM for Purchase?

Your share is the price minus what the reverse mortgage can lend, plus any costs you don't roll into the loan. Three things set what the loan can lend:

  • Your age. The older the youngest borrower, the more the loan covers.
  • Interest rates at the time.
  • The home's value. For 2026, the loan is figured on value up to $1,249,125.

HECM for Purchase requirements

  • The youngest borrower is 62 or older.
  • You move in within 60 days and live there as your main home.
  • You meet with a HUD-approved counselor. The counselor walks you through the loan and your other choices.
  • Your share comes from your own money, such as savings or the sale of your home.
  • The home is finished and ready to live in. A condo has to be in an FHA-approved building.
  • The lender checks that you can keep up with taxes, insurance and upkeep. It may set aside part of the loan to pay those bills.

What you still pay after you move in

No monthly mortgage payment is required. You still pay property taxes, homeowners insurance, flood insurance if the home needs it, HOA dues, and repairs. If you fall behind on these, the loan can come due, and you could lose the home.

The loan balance grows over time, because interest and FHA mortgage insurance are added to it each month. So the equity left for you or your heirs shrinks as the years go by. You can make payments if you want to, but you don't have to. Here is what happens to a reverse mortgage after death.

Retiring to Myrtle Beach? South Carolina's tax breaks help

If you're moving to the Grand Strand from another state, a few South Carolina rules make the monthly math easier once you own the home:

  • Your home gets the lower tax rate. As your main home, it's assessed at 4% instead of 6%, and it's exempt from school operating taxes. You apply with the county.
  • The 65+ homestead exemption. At 65 or older, after you've lived in South Carolina for a year, the first $50,000 of your main home's value is exempt from property taxes. Apply to the county auditor, and to the town or city if the home is inside one, before July 16 for that tax year.
  • Social Security isn't taxed by South Carolina. That leaves more room for the taxes, insurance and upkeep you still pay with a reverse mortgage.

Buying close to the water? Flood insurance may be part of your costs. See flood insurance in Myrtle Beach. Looking at a condo? The building must be FHA-approved; see Myrtle Beach condo financing.

Is a HECM for Purchase a good idea?

It fits well if you plan to stay in the home for years, you want to keep savings for health care, travel or family, and you are comfortable with the balance growing.

It fits less well if you may move again soon, or if leaving the full home to your heirs matters most to you. For a home you already own, compare a reverse mortgage with a home equity loan.
