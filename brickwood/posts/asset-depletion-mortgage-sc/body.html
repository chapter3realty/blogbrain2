How the asset depletion math works

Fannie Mae calls this "employment-related assets as qualifying income". The lender works out your "net documented assets" and spreads them over the loan:

  1. Add up eligible assets. Retirement accounts you can withdraw from freely, and lump-sum retirement or severance payouts.
  2. Subtract any penalty. If withdrawing everything today would trigger a penalty, that amount comes off.
  3. Subtract the cash you use to buy. Your down payment, closing costs and required reserves come off.
  4. Divide by the number of months in the loan. For a 30-year loan, that is 360 months. The result is your monthly qualifying income.
Fannie Mae's own example

Say $350,000 is left after any withdrawal penalty and the cash you use to buy. The lender divides it by 360 months. That is $972.22 a month of qualifying income.

That income is added to any Social Security, pension or other income you already have. Together they are compared with your housing cost and debts, the same way a paycheck would be.

Accounts must be in your name, or the co-owner must also be a borrower on the loan. If you already take regular retirement distributions that are large enough, those can count as income on their own, and you may not need this rule at all.

What an asset depletion loan can be used for

  • Purchase or a limited cash-out refinance. It cannot be used for a cash-out refinance.
  • The home you live in or a second home. Not an investment property.
  • A smaller loan than usual. Fannie Mae caps the loan at a lower share of the price than on a standard loan. The cap is higher when the asset owner is 62 or older at closing. We tell you the exact figure for your purchase.

Because the loan must be smaller, buyers using this rule make a larger down payment from the same savings. The steps above already take that cash out, so we plan the down payment and the qualifying income together. The rule works for a beach second home too; see second home vs investment property loans.

Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac and other lenders

Fannie Mae's rule is strict about which money counts. Money in a bank or brokerage account, such as cash from selling a business, does not count under it unless it came from a retirement account or payout. That money has other routes.

Programs outside Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac usually cost more. BrickWood is a broker, so we run your accounts through each route and show you which gives the strongest result.

Other paths may fit better:

  • Self-employed with strong deposits: a bank statement loan.
  • Buying a rental: a DSCR loan, which qualifies you on the rent.
  • Age 62 or older and buying a home to live in: a reverse mortgage for purchase. No monthly mortgage payment is required, but you still pay taxes, insurance and upkeep, and the loan is repaid when you sell, move out or die.

For other kinds of income lenders count, see can I get a mortgage without a job.

What you need to show

  • Your most recent monthly, quarterly or annual statement for each retirement account, showing what it holds.
  • For a lump-sum retirement or severance payout: the employer's distribution letter or Form 1099-R, and proof it was deposited.
  • Proof you can withdraw the money now.
  • No pay stubs and no work history.
