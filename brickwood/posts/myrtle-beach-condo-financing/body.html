Financing a condo in Myrtle Beach comes down to two questions: do you qualify, and does the building? In 2026, Fannie Mae changed the rules for the building part. For the oceanfront towers and resort-style buildings along the Grand Strand, some of those changes help and some hurt. Here's what changed and what it means if you're buying.

What changed for condo loans in 2026

1. The quick building check is gone. Lenders used to be able to approve many condo buildings with a short checklist, called a Limited Review. For loan applications dated on or after August 3, 2026, that option is gone. A finished building with more than 10 units, which covers most oceanfront towers, now needs a full review of its budget, savings, repairs and insurance, FHA approval, or a review by Fannie Mae itself.

2. The 50% investor limit is gone, for finished buildings. Fannie Mae used to require that at least half the units in a building be owned by people who live there or use them as second homes, before it would back a loan on a rental unit. That rule is gone for established buildings. That's good news on the Grand Strand, where many buildings are full of rentals. A building can still fail if it runs like a hotel. A brand-new building still needs at least half its units sold to people who will live there or use them as second homes.

3. HOAs must save more. The HOA has to put at least 10% of the dues it collects each year into savings for big repairs. For loan applications dated on or after January 4, 2027, that rises to 15%.

Small buildings are simpler. Buildings of two to four units skip the full review, and so do five- to ten-unit buildings that aren't part of a bigger complex.

Condo-hotels and buildings with a front desk

This is the big one at the beach. Fannie Mae won't back a loan on a unit in a building that operates like a hotel, even though each unit has its own owner. Signs a Myrtle Beach building may be treated as a hotel:

  • a front desk that checks in guests, or daily cleaning service;
  • a rule that owners must put their unit in the rental program;
  • owners must split rental income with the HOA or a rental company;
  • "hotel" or "resort" in its name, or marketing as a hotel or investment.

Renting out your own unit is fine. It's the building's setup that matters.

Other reasons a condo can't get a regular loan

A building that fails Fannie Mae's rules is called non-warrantable. Besides hotel-style operations, the usual reasons are:

  • Big repairs with no money set aside. Problems like a failing sea wall, leaks, or a failed structural inspection, or more than $10,000 a unit of unpaid repairs due within a year.
  • A special assessment for those repairs. If the extra bill pays for a serious repair that isn't fixed yet, the building can't pass.
  • One owner has too many units. In a building of 21 or more units, no single person or company can own more than 20% of them.
  • Too much commercial space. Shops, restaurants or a hotel section can't take up more than 35% of the building.
  • Owners behind on dues. No more than 15% of units can be two months or more behind on HOA dues.

Renting out your Myrtle Beach condo

Many buyers plan to rent their unit some weeks. A few local things to budget for:

  • Lodging taxes. A short-term rental inside the City of Myrtle Beach collects about 13% in lodging taxes on each stay. For lodging taxes, South Carolina counts any stay under 90 days in a row as short-term.
  • North Myrtle Beach. The city requires a business license to rent short-term. For now there is no separate rental permit.
  • How you'll use it changes the loan. If you'll stay there part of the year and keep control of the bookings, it can be a second home. See second home vs investment property in Myrtle Beach.

Insurance on a beach condo

If the building sits in a high-risk flood zone, the lender looks for a master flood policy the HOA carries on the building. Myrtle Beach and North Myrtle Beach get discounts on federal flood insurance; see flood insurance in Myrtle Beach.

Tips before you make an offer

  • Ask the listing agent whether recent buyers in the building used regular loans. It's a quick hint about whether the building passes.
  • Ask about special assessments and planned repairs.
  • Check whether the building has a front desk or a required rental program.
  • Get pre-qualified, and tell your loan officer it's a condo. Once you're under contract and apply, the lender reviews the building.
