Do you have to use the builder's lender?

No. When a builder owns part of a lender, federal law allows that setup only if the builder doesn't make you use the lender to get the home, and gives you a written notice of the relationship and the lender's estimated charges. Any discount for using that lender has to be optional, a true discount, and not made up through higher costs somewhere else in the closing.

Many preferred lenders aren't owned by the builder at all. Either way, the incentive is an offer to weigh, not a rule you have to follow.

The incentive is there to steer you to the builder's lender. Turning it down is always your choice.

How builder incentives work

The offer usually looks like one of these:

  • a credit toward your closing costs;
  • money to lower your interest rate for a while, called a buydown;
  • upgrades in the house, like flooring or appliances.

Fannie Mae caps how much a builder or seller can pay toward your closing costs. The cap depends on how you'll use the home and on your down payment, and the money can't go toward the down payment itself. So a very large incentive may not all be usable.

For most home loans, the lender, or the broker who took your application, must send you a Loan Estimate within three business days of your application. It's a standard three-page form, so two lenders' offers line up line by line. Ask for one from the builder's lender and one from us, and lay them side by side.

An example. A builder in Carolina Forest offers a $10,000 closing cost credit if the buyers use its lender. The buyers ask BrickWood for a Loan Estimate too. Sometimes a lower rate somewhere else is worth more than the credit over the years they plan to own the home. Either way, they choose with the numbers in front of them, not the sales pitch.

Ask about the rate lock

A new home can take months to build. A rate lock holds your interest rate until closing, and longer locks can cost more. A builder's lender may offer a long lock as part of the deal, which can be worth a lot if rates rise during the build. Ask each lender how long it can lock and what that costs, and compare that too.

Read the financing section of the builder's contract

New construction contracts are written by the builder. Before you sign, look for:

  • The incentive terms. What exactly you get, and what happens to it if you use another lender.
  • Financing deadlines. How long you have to get approved. Get pre-qualified before you sign, so the clock isn't a surprise.
  • The deposit. When your earnest money becomes non-refundable.

Your real estate agent or attorney can walk you through the contract. Ask us how long a loan like yours usually takes to close, so you can set that next to the contract's deadline.
