What changed for condo loans in 2026

Fannie Mae removed the Limited Review option for condo buildings. Limited Review let a lender approve many established buildings with a shorter check. That shortcut is gone for loan applications dated on or after August 3, 2026.

Today a conventional loan on a unit in an established building with more than 10 units goes through one of three paths:

PathWhat it means for you
Full ReviewYour lender reviews the building itself: the HOA budget, reserves, unpaid dues, repairs, insurance, lawsuits and how the building is used.
FHA project approvalIf HUD itself has approved the building for FHA loans, a conventional lender can rely on that approval.
Fannie Mae review (PERS)Fannie Mae reviews the building directly. This is used for new buildings and special cases.

The good news: for established buildings under Full Review, Fannie Mae also dropped its 50% limit on units owned by investors. A finished building with many rentals is no longer turned down for that reason alone. A new or newly converted building still needs at least half its units sold to buyers who will live there or use them as second homes.

Smaller buildings are simpler. Fannie Mae waives the building review, apart from a few basic requirements, for detached condos, buildings of two to four units, and five- to ten-unit buildings that are not part of a larger development or master association.

What makes a condo building fail the review

A building fails when Fannie Mae sees risk in the building itself. These are the reasons we see most often, from Fannie Mae's Selling Guide.

Repairs the building has not paid for

A building that needs critical repairs is ineligible until the work is done. Critical repairs include problems that could cause a major system to fail within a year, mold or water getting into the building, advanced deterioration, and a failed mandatory structural inspection. Unfunded repairs costing more than $10,000 per unit that should be done within 12 months also count. Repairs owners make inside their own units, and work already paid for by a special assessment, do not. Among the items Fannie Mae lists to consider are sea walls, elevators, waterproofing, stairwells, balconies, foundations, electrical systems and parking structures, which is why coastal buildings get a close read.

Routine upkeep paid from the normal budget does not count. Damage limited to one or a few units, which does not affect the whole building, does not count either.

A special assessment for a critical repair

A special assessment is an extra charge the HOA bills owners for a big project. Your lender reviews each one: what it pays for, when it was approved and whether the work has started, its total, how much is still owed and when it will be paid off. If the assessment is for a critical repair and the problem is not fixed yet, the building is ineligible.

Thin reserves or owners behind on dues

Under Full Review, the HOA budget must put at least 10% of its assessment income toward replacement reserves, the savings for roofs, elevators and other big items. For loan applications dated on or after January 4, 2027, that minimum rises to 15%. A professional reserve study can stand in for that figure if it shows the building is adequately funded. No more than 15% of the units may be 60 or more days behind on their HOA dues.

One owner with too many units

In a building of 21 or more units, one person or company may own no more than 20% of the units. In a building of 11 to 20 units, the limit is 2 units.

Too much commercial space

No more than 35% of the building may be commercial space, such as shops, restaurants or a hotel section.

An evacuation order

A building under a full or partial evacuation order for an unsafe condition is ineligible until the condition is fixed and the building is declared safe.

Condotels and buildings with a rental desk

Fannie Mae will not buy a loan on a unit in a building that is run like a hotel, even when each unit has its own owner. A building can be treated as a hotel when it has any of these:

  • an HOA licensed as a hotel, motel, resort or hospitality business;
  • a rule that owners must put their unit in a rental pool, or share rental profits with the HOA or a rental company;
  • hotel services such as registration services, daily cleaning or central key systems;
  • "hotel", "motel" or "resort" in its name, unless that is only history;
  • marketing as a hotel, motel, resort or investment opportunity.

Owning a unit you rent out yourself is not the problem. The building's setup is. A building where owners rent on their own, with no required pool and no front desk, can still pass.

How FHA treats condos

An FHA loan on a condo needs the building to be FHA-approved, or the single unit to get a Single-Unit Approval, which still has to meet HUD's building standards. A building meant for rental for transient or hotel purposes cannot get FHA approval. If you plan to use an FHA loan, we check the building's approval status first. See our FHA loans page for the loan itself.

What to do if the building does not qualify

A building that fails Fannie Mae's rules is called non-warrantable. You can still finance it. BrickWood is a mortgage broker, so we can take the loan to lenders who keep loans in their own portfolio or offer programs outside Fannie Mae's rules. These loans usually ask for a larger down payment and cost more than a conventional loan. We show you the terms side by side before you decide.

A lender can also ask Fannie Mae for an exception when a building misses one rule but is otherwise sound. That takes time, so it works best when we start before you sign a contract.

Two related options:

  • If you will rent the unit, a DSCR loan qualifies you on the rent instead of your income.
  • If you are self-employed, a bank statement loan uses your deposits instead of tax returns.

What we check before you make an offer

  1. The building's name and status. We look it up in Fannie Mae's project system and HUD's FHA list.
  2. The HOA's papers. We request the condo questionnaire, budget, reserve study and insurance from the HOA or its manager.
  3. Repairs and assessments. We ask about inspections, planned work and every special assessment.
  4. The rental setup. We check for a rental pool, a front desk or hotel services.
  5. The right loan. We tell you which loans the building qualifies for and what each one needs from you.

Doing this before the offer means your contract's financing deadline is not spent waiting on a building review.
