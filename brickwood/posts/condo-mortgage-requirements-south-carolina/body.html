To get a regular mortgage on a condo, both you and the building have to qualify. In 2026, Fannie Mae changed the rules for the building part. Some buildings will find it harder to pass, and some will find it easier. Here's what changed and what it means for you as a buyer.

What changed for condo loans in 2026

1. The quick building check is gone. Lenders used to be able to approve many condo buildings with a short checklist, called a Limited Review. For loan applications dated on or after August 3, 2026, that option is gone. A finished building with more than 10 units now needs a full review of its budget, savings, repairs and insurance, FHA approval, or a review by Fannie Mae itself.

2. The 50% investor limit is gone, for finished buildings. Fannie Mae used to require that at least half the units in a building be owned by people who live there or use them as second homes, before it would back a loan on a rental unit. That rule is gone for established buildings. A building full of rentals can still fail if it runs like a hotel. A brand-new building still needs at least half its units sold to people who will live there or use them as second homes.

3. HOAs must save more. The HOA has to put at least 10% of the dues it collects each year into savings for big repairs. For loan applications dated on or after January 4, 2027, that rises to 15%.

Small buildings are simpler. Buildings of two to four units skip the full review, and so do five- to ten-unit buildings that aren't part of a bigger complex.

Why some condos can't get a regular loan

When a building fails Fannie Mae's rules, it's called non-warrantable. These are the usual reasons:

  • Big repairs with no money set aside. Problems like a failing sea wall, leaks, or a failed structural inspection, or more than $10,000 a unit of unpaid repairs due within a year.
  • A special assessment for those repairs. An extra bill the HOA charges owners. If it pays for a serious repair that isn't fixed yet, the building can't pass.
  • It's run like a hotel. More on this below.
  • One owner has too many units. In a building of 21 or more units, no single person or company can own more than 20% of them.
  • Too much commercial space. Shops, restaurants or a hotel section can't take up more than 35% of the building.
  • Owners behind on dues. No more than 15% of units can be two months or more behind on HOA dues.

Condotels and buildings with a front desk

Fannie Mae won't back a loan on a unit in a building that operates like a hotel, even though each unit has its own owner. Signs a building may be treated as a hotel:

  • a front desk that checks in guests, or daily cleaning service;
  • a rule that owners must put their unit in the rental program;
  • owners must split rental income with the HOA or a rental company;
  • "hotel" or "resort" in its name, or marketing as a hotel or investment.

Renting out your own unit is fine. It's the building's setup that matters.

What if the condo doesn't qualify?

You can still buy it. A non-warrantable condo just needs a different kind of loan. BrickWood is a mortgage broker, so we can take your loan to lenders that make condo loans outside Fannie Mae's rules. The terms are different from a regular loan, so your loan officer will walk you through the options.

Other paths:

  • FHA: works if the building is FHA-approved, or if the single unit can get FHA approval on its own. See our FHA loans.
  • DSCR: buying it as a rental? A DSCR loan qualifies you on the rent.

Tips before you make an offer

  • Ask the listing agent whether recent buyers in the building used regular loans. It's a quick hint about whether the building passes.
  • Ask about special assessments and planned repairs.
  • Check whether the building has a front desk or a required rental program.
  • Get pre-qualified, and tell your loan officer it's a condo. Once you're under contract and apply, the lender reviews the building.
