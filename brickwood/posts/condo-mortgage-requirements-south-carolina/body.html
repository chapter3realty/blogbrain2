What changed for condo loans in 2026

Fannie Mae removed the Limited Review option for condo buildings. Limited Review let a lender approve many established buildings with a shorter check. Lenders can no longer use it for loan applications dated on or after August 3, 2026. Freddie Mac, the other company that buys conventional loans, has its own condo rules, and we check the building against both.

Today a loan sold to Fannie Mae on a unit in an established building with more than 10 units goes through one of three paths:

PathWhat it means for you
Full ReviewYour lender reviews the building itself: the HOA budget, reserves, unpaid dues, repairs, insurance, lawsuits and how the building is used.
FHA project approvalIf HUD itself has approved the building for FHA loans, a conventional lender can rely on that approval.
A review by Fannie Mae itselfFannie Mae reviews the building directly. This is used for new buildings and special cases.

For established buildings under Full Review, Fannie Mae also dropped its 50% limit on units owned by investors. A finished building with many rentals is no longer turned down for that reason alone. A new or newly converted building still needs at least half its units sold to buyers who will live there or use them as second homes.

Smaller buildings are simpler. Fannie Mae waives the building review, apart from a few basic requirements, for detached condos, buildings of two to four units, and five- to ten-unit buildings that are not part of a larger development or master association.

Does this apply to my loan?

Yes, whether you will live in the condo, use it as a second home or rent it out. The building review depends on the building, not on how you use your unit. How you use the unit changes the loan itself: the down payment, and whether the rent can help you qualify. Our guide to second home vs investment property loans explains that part.

What makes a condo building fail the review

Under Fannie Mae's rules, a building fails for any of these reasons.

Repairs the building has not paid for

A building is ineligible while it needs critical repairs: problems that could make a major system fail within a year, mold or water coming in, advanced deterioration, or a failed mandatory structural inspection. Unpaid repairs over $10,000 a unit that are due within 12 months count too. Fannie Mae lists sea walls, elevators, balconies, waterproofing and parking structures among the items to consider. Routine upkeep, repairs owners make inside their own units, work already paid for by a special assessment, and damage to only one or a few units do not count.

A special assessment for a critical repair

A special assessment is an extra charge the HOA bills owners for a big project. Your lender reviews each one: what it pays for, when it was approved and whether the work has started, its total, how much is still owed and when it will be paid off. If the assessment is for a critical repair and the problem is not fixed yet, the building is ineligible.

Thin reserves or owners behind on dues

Under Full Review, the HOA budget must put at least 10% of its assessment income toward replacement reserves, the savings for roofs, elevators and other big items. For loan applications dated on or after January 4, 2027, that minimum rises to 15%. A professional reserve study can be used instead if it shows the building is adequately funded. No more than 15% of the units may be 60 or more days behind on their HOA dues.

An evacuation order

A building under a full or partial evacuation order for an unsafe condition is ineligible until the condition is fixed and the building is declared safe.

Condotels and buildings with a rental desk

Fannie Mae will not buy a loan on a unit in a building that is run like a hotel, even when each unit has its own owner. A building can be treated as a hotel when it has any of these:

  • an HOA licensed as a hotel, motel, resort or hospitality business;
  • a rule that owners must put their unit in a rental pool, or share rental profits with the HOA or a rental company;
  • hotel services such as registration services, daily cleaning or central key systems;
  • "hotel", "motel" or "resort" in its name, unless that is only history;
  • marketing as a hotel, motel, resort or investment opportunity.

Owning a unit you rent out yourself is not the problem. The building's setup is. Our second home guide explains how a rental program changes your own loan. A building where owners rent on their own, with no required pool and no front desk, can still pass.

How FHA treats condos

An FHA loan on a condo needs the building to be FHA-approved, or the single unit to get a Single-Unit Approval, which still has to meet HUD's building standards. A building meant for rental for transient or hotel purposes cannot get FHA approval. If you plan to use an FHA loan, we check the building's approval status first. See our FHA loans page for the loan itself.

What to do if the building does not qualify

A building that fails Fannie Mae's rules is called non-warrantable. You can still finance it. BrickWood is a mortgage broker, so we can take the loan to lenders who keep loans in their own portfolio or offer programs outside Fannie Mae's rules. Their terms differ from a conventional loan, so we show you the options side by side before you decide.

A lender can also ask Fannie Mae for an exception when a building misses one rule but is otherwise sound. That takes time, so it works best when we start before you sign a contract.

Two related options:

  • If you will rent the unit, a DSCR loan qualifies you on the rent instead of your income.
  • If you are self-employed, a bank statement loan uses your deposits instead of tax returns.
